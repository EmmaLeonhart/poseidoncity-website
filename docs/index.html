POSEIDON

A self-governing technology haven on the Pitcairn Islands, built legally and with the islanders.

The idea

Charter cities, seasteads and space settlements all run into the same wall: they have no legal standing as a jurisdiction. Project Poseidon starts from a place that already has one. The Pitcairn Islands are a British overseas territory in the South Pacific with about 40 residents, listed by the UN as a non-self-governing territory. That makes Pitcairn the smallest existing jurisdiction where a committed community can build something new through ordinary residency and elections.

The long-term goal

Free association with New Zealand, the arrangement the Cook Islands and Niue already have. Pitcairn already uses the New Zealand dollar, and the UK spends about $5 million a year administering it. Free association would give the island broad self-government while easing that cost for the UK.

There is precedent on both counts. The UK has handed Pacific territories to New Zealand and Australia before: the Cook Islands and Niue in 1901, Norfolk Island in 1914, Tokelau in 1926, the Cocos (Keeling) Islands in 1955 and Christmas Island in 1958. And size is no barrier to a vote on status: in 1984 the Cocos (Keeling) Islands, with 261 registered voters, held a UN-observed referendum that offered free association alongside independence and integration.

How we get there

  1. Bridgehead. A few settlers live on the island, become part of the community and gain permanent residency.
  2. Partnership. More settlers arrive, and we invest in the island's own economy: agriculture, honey production, light industry and tourism accommodation.
  3. Representation. With a few dozen residents, the community has a real voice in the island's elections and can openly bring in larger investment.
  4. Growth and association. Build out the haven, then pursue free association with New Zealand through a referendum.

What it makes possible

A jurisdiction open to frontier technology: AI and data hosting, biotechnology research, and a registry for seasteads and space ventures that need a legitimate flag. Original islanders would share in the proceeds.

It also puts Pitcairn on the map of the internet itself. The first undersea cable between South America and the Asia-Pacific, Humboldt, is due in 2027 on a route from Chile to Tahiti that passes about 140 km from Pitcairn. A branch to the island would give a Pitcairn data centre fibre to two continents. Read the cable plan →

Frequently asked questions

Will newcomers outnumber the islanders?

No. The first stage is a handful of people who live on the island, join the community and earn its trust over years, while investing in what islanders already do: honey, agriculture, light industry and tourism. Growth comes later and openly, and original islanders would share in the proceeds.

Doesn't a UK-appointed governor hold all the power?

The Governor has reserve powers in theory, but the UK rarely intervenes. Day-to-day decisions are made by the Mayor and the Island Council, which residents elect. Free association with New Zealand would move most remaining powers to the island.

The island is tiny, with little water and a small economy. Can this really work?

Charter cities have been built in harsher places, and Pitcairn's small size is exactly what makes a community-scale start possible. Practical limits such as water, transport (today via Tahiti and Mangareva) and internet (Starlink only) are part of the plan we are working through; a direct shipping arrangement and, later, an airstrip are among the options.

Where will the money come from? Have you raised any yet?

Fundraising is at an early stage. The first stage is small: an estimated $50,000 or less. A jurisdiction with real legal standing is something no other charter-city project can offer investors.

What is the internet like, and could Pitcairn host data centres?

Today the island is online through Starlink satellite internet; there is no undersea fibre cable. Satellite is enough for early, light workloads. A Pitcairn data centre is part of the plan, and a fibre link could follow by making Pitcairn a stop on the new cable route between South America and the Asia-Pacific (the cable plan).

Can outsiders own land or live there?

Pitcairn's rules on foreign land ownership are not fully clear, but the island has sought foreign investment several times before. Residency starts with a visa and leads to permanent residency, normally after about two years; permanent residents can vote in island elections.

Will the economy rely on gambling, drugs or similar?

No. The focus is technology, research, and the island's own agriculture, industry and tourism.

Has anyone approached Pitcairn with an idea like this before?

Yes. On 12 January 2020 the Pitcairn Island Council met representatives of the cryptocurrency network TRON, who proposed working with the island on crypto regulation and private ventures. The Council let them present the idea to the whole community (approved Council minutes). The deal did not go ahead, but it shows the island is open to hearing such proposals. We think a plan built around the community, with many uses rather than crypto alone, has a better chance.

What if Pitcairn doesn't work out?

Other small territories with similar legal standing, such as Niue and the Cocos (Keeling) Islands, have been considered as alternatives.
